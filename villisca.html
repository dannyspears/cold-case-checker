The Villisca axe murders: eight people, one night, a 114-year-old question.

On the night of June 9–10, 1912, eight people were killed with an axe in their beds in a small Iowa town, six of them children. A minister was tried twice and not convicted. A century of theories has not produced a verdict.

Case file at a glance

Villisca is a case with almost no physical evidence and a huge paper trail. The 2017 book "The Man from the Train" argues the killer was a serial offender who struck families near railroad lines, which turns a one-town mystery into a records problem.

Victims8, six of them children
DateJune 9–10, 1912
PlaceVillisca, Iowa
WeaponAxe (the family's own)
ChargedRev. George Kelly (acquitted)
StatusUnsolved
  1. The family and two guests were killed in their beds.Josiah and Sarah Moore, their four children, and two overnight guests died after a church program. The killer seems to have waited in the attic.
  2. The scene was ruined within hours.Townspeople crowded the house before the National Guard secured it, and some took souvenirs. That wrecked fingerprint and trace evidence.
  3. One man was tried twice.Rev. George Kelly signed a confession in 1917 then recanted. His first trial ended in a hung jury and he was acquitted at the second.
  4. A newer theory points to a serial offender.A 2017 book links the case to axe murders of other families across several states.

What happened

Dates come from court and news records. Details of the night come from period accounts and are partly disputed.

June 9, 1912 · evening

Children's Day

The Moore family attends a Presbyterian Children's Day program. Two neighbor girls, Lena and Ina Stillinger, come home with the children to stay the night.

June 9–10 · overnight

The killings

All eight are killed with an axe that belonged to Josiah Moore. The killer appears to have entered the house, waited in the attic (investigators found two spent cigarettes there), and covered every mirror in the house.

June 10 · morning

Discovery

Neighbor Mary Peckham notices the house is quiet and alerts others. Josiah's brother, Ross Moore, and Constable Henry Horton enter and find the victims. The axe, found in the guest room, had been wiped. A crowd gathers before the National Guard secures the scene.

1912

Suspects and detectives

Local and state authorities pursue several men. Detective James Wilkerson of the Burns agency builds a theory involving state senator Frank F. Jones, Josiah's former employer. A grand jury declines to indict William Mansfield, who had a payroll alibi in Illinois.

Late 1912

Henry Lee Moore

Henry Lee Moore, unrelated to the family, is convicted of axe murders of his mother and grandmother months later. He is "heavily theorized" as the killer but nothing proves it.

1917–1918

The Kelly trials

Rev. George Kelly, who left town on an early train before the bodies were found, signs a confession in 1917 but recants. His first trial ends in a hung jury; he is acquitted at the second, in November 1917.

1990s

The house becomes a landmark

Martha Linn buys the house in 1994 and restores it to its 1912 condition. It later becomes a tourist and ghost-hunting site.

2017

The Man from the Train

Bill James and Rachel McCarthy suggest the killer was Paul Mueller, a German immigrant tied to axe killings of families in several states.

Latest theory: Paul Mueller and "The Man from the Train"

No suspect is currently named by authorities. The newest widely discussed theory is a 2017 book that treats Villisca as one of many family murders.

Not an accusation. The theory is an argument by two authors, not a finding by police or a court. Mueller is long dead.

The theory

  • Bill James and Rachel McCarthy argue that the same man killed families with an axe across several states, usually near railroad lines, over a span of years.
  • They name Paul Mueller, a German immigrant, and link him to an 1897 axe murder in Massachusetts.
  • Villisca is, in their view, one of the best-documented cases in a larger pattern.

The claims and how strong they are

ClaimSourceStatusOur rating
Many family axe murders across states share a method and railroad accessJames and McCarthy, 2017Based on newspaper and record research; reviewed by commentators.Plausible, circumstantial

What would settle it

  1. Check the other scenes. A published, source-by-source comparison of the family murders, with dates and rail timetables.
  2. Record Mueller's movements. Employment, rail, and immigration records for June 1912.
  3. Examine the axe for any retained trace, though handling over 114 years makes that unlikely.
Cold Case Checker's assessment

A strong pattern idea, an unproven name

We think the serial-offender idea is the most useful frame, since it turns a hopeless local puzzle into a searchable records problem. We put the chance that Villisca belongs to a multi-state series at roughly 35%, and the chance that Paul Mueller specifically was the killer at roughly 10%. A dated record placing him in or near Villisca on June 9, 1912 would raise that sharply.

Our hypothesis

Editorial reading of public records. A working theory, not a finding, and it names no individual.

Working theory · confidence stated per point

A transient offender with an axe, and a paper trail that has not been fully searched

  1. The killer was not a local with a grudge. The family was targeted at random, as far as anyone can tell. Two overnight guests died. A motive-driven killer would not have killed the neighbors' children.
  2. He used the house's own tools and left no trace. The axe came from the family's property. Covered mirrors and a wiped axe suggest a controlled scene.
  3. He did not stay. No suspect in Villisca stayed in the area, which matches a traveling offender more than a local one.
  4. Records, not forensics, are the path. Digitized newspapers and rail timetables have made multi-state searches possible in a way they were not before.

Missing links

Questions raised by gaps in the public record.

1. A cross-case database

A public table comparing every proposed family axe murder: date, place, method, rail access, and evidence.

2. Mueller's whereabouts

A sourced timeline of Paul Mueller in 1912, from employment, rail, and immigration records.

3. The 1917 confession

The full interrogation record and who was present, to judge how reliable it was.

4. The attic and the cigarettes

Where the cigarettes ended up and whether anything was kept.

5. The axe

Whether any retained trace exists, and a documented chain of custody since 1912.

6. The Wilkerson files

The Burns agency detective's files on Jones and Mansfield; their location and availability.

7. The covered mirrors

Whether similar practices occur at the other proposed scenes.

8. Digitized newspapers

A systematic search of 1910s newspapers for similar overnight family murders along rail lines.

Solved cases

Golden State Killer

Joseph DeAngelo was arrested in 2018 after DNA matched relatives in a public database. The lesson: new methods can solve a decades-old series, but they need DNA that Villisca does not have.

Boston Strangler (Mary Sullivan)

In 2013, DNA from a 1964 victim was matched through a relative to Albert DeSalvo. The lesson: kept evidence matters, and 1912 evidence was not preserved.

What differs here

A 1912 case has no DNA and no living witnesses. Success depends on documents.

Remembering the victims

Eight people, six of them children, were killed in their sleep. The house is now a tourist attraction, which many descendants and residents find hard to accept.
