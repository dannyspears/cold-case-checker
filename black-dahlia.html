The Black Dahlia: 79 years of confessions, one case file still sealed.

On January 15, 1947, a woman's body was found in a vacant lot in Los Angeles. She was Elizabeth Short, 22. Hundreds of people have confessed, dozens have been suspected, and the LAPD has never closed the case. In 2026 new claims put an old suspect back at the center.

Case file at a glance

The Black Dahlia case is stuck on records, not clues. The key items are a sealed autopsy report, grand-jury files, and whatever physical evidence the LAPD still holds. Until those are opened and retested, every theory is guesswork.

What happened

Dates come from police, coroner, and press records. We keep graphic detail to what is needed to understand the case.

July 1946

Short moves to Los Angeles

Elizabeth Short, born July 29, 1924 in Boston, spends her last six months in southern California, working odd jobs and waitressing.

Jan 9, 1947

The last confirmed day

Robert "Red" Manley, a 25-year-old salesman she had been dating, drives her from San Diego to Los Angeles. He says he dropped her at the Biltmore Hotel, where she planned to meet a sister. A 1997 Los Angeles Times article questioned whether she was actually seen at the Biltmore. Her whereabouts for the next six days are unknown.

Jan 15, 1947

Discovery

Her body is found in a vacant lot on South Norton Avenue in Leimert Park. It had been severed at the waist, washed, and posed. Detectives find a heel print and a cement sack nearby. The autopsy, performed Jan 16 by coroner Frederick Newbarr, gives the cause as hemorrhage from facial wounds and shock from blows to the head and face.

Jan 1947 · Identification

FBI fingerprints

Her prints, sent to the FBI, match a 1943 arrest record. Reporters reach her mother in Boston before police do, and tell her false details to draw out information.

Jan 21–29, 1947

The "Black Dahlia Avenger"

On Jan 21 a caller tells a Los Angeles Examiner editor that souvenirs will arrive. On Jan 24 an envelope arrives with her birth certificate, business cards, photos, and an address book containing the name Mark Hansen, all wiped with gasoline. (Accounts differ on which paper received it.) A handbag and shoe turn up two miles from the scene. A Jan 26 letter promises a surrender on Jan 29; no one appears. A cut-and-paste letter on Jan 29 says the killer changed his mind.

1947 · The investigation

Confessions and suspects

Police receive about 60 confessions in the first weeks and interview roughly 150 men. Many confessions are false; several confessors are charged with obstruction. A $10,000 reward is offered.

Sept 1949 – 1951

Grand jury and the Jemison list

A grand jury examines LAPD homicide failures, including this case. A 1951 list names 22 persons of interest. In 1949–50, the DA's office wiretaps a physician named in later accusations, a file that surfaces in 2003.

1990s – 2017

Books and claims

Janice Knowlton says in the early 1990s, from "recovered memories," that her father did it. In 2003 Steve Hodel publishes his accusation against his father. In 2017 Piu Eatwell's book implicates Leslie Dillon. Each claim is disputed.

Dec 2025 – 2026

New claims

In Dec 2025 investigator Alex Baber names Marvin Margolis, also tied to the Zodiac case. In May 2026 an LAPD detective says he is looking at Baber's 1943 fingerprint card. In July 2026 the "Deconstructing Dahlia" team says it found nearly 40 blood samples in a motel room it dismantled. A petition asks the LAPD to release Short's full autopsy report. A book by William J. Mann, published in 2026, calls Margolis the one suspect he could not rule out.

The victim

Elizabeth Short was 22. She is better known by a nickname the press gave her than by her own name.

From an LAPD bulletin, Jan 15, 1947. Public domain per Wikimedia Commons.

Died

Elizabeth Short, 22

Born July 29, 1924 in Hyde Park, Boston, the third of five daughters. She worked as a waitress and is often called an aspiring actress, though no acting credits are known. She is buried at Mountain View Cemetery in Oakland, California.

Rumors about her private life, repeated for decades, are unsubstantiated. Her family and others who want a photo added, changed, or removed can use the contact form at the bottom of this page.

Suspects

Police interviewed about 150 men early on, and the 1951 list named 22. These are the names most often raised.

No one has been charged. People listed here have not been convicted. Several were cleared by police and are deceased. Many claims come from authors, not from official findings.
No free photo found
Unverified, newest

Marvin Margolis (b. 1925, d. 1993)

Why named: One of Short's boyfriends, on the 1951 list. Navy medical corpsman at Okinawa, discharged 50% disabled; USC pre-med student. Lived for a time in the same building as Short and first understated how well he knew her. Author William J. Mann calls him the one suspect he could not exonerate. See the full section.

Against: His recently married wife gave him an alibi, and police eliminated him. Claims tying him to the Zodiac are unverified.

George Hodel, c. 1950. Photo: Steve Hodel, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons. Displayed cropped to fit. Steve Hodel is the person who accuses his father.

Cleared by LAPD; disputed

George Hodel

Why named: A physician accused of molesting his daughter in 1949 (he was acquitted) and wiretapped by the DA in 1949–50. A 2003 disclosure of grand jury notes quotes him joking about the killing. His son, former LAPD detective Steve Hodel, says he did it.

Against: LAPD cleared him after a month-long investigation and could not show he and Short ever met. Mann (2026) argues no convincing evidence they met.

No free photo found
Disputed

Leslie Dillon

Why named: A former mortician who wrote letters to the LAPD in 1949 blaming a friend. A police psychiatrist believed he was the killer. Piu Eatwell's 2017 book supports this.

Against: No solid evidence. Researchers cite DA files placing him in San Francisco.

No free photo found
Dismissed by LAPD

Robert "Red" Manley

Why named: One of the last people known to have seen her alive; he drove her to Los Angeles on Jan 9, 1947.

Against: Parts of his account were corroborated and he passed several polygraph tests.

No free photo found
Cleared by LAPD

Mark Hansen

Why named: Nightclub owner whose name was in the address book in the package. Short had stayed at his house. A friend said Short rejected his advances.

Against: No solid evidence linking him to the crime.

No free photo found
Newer claim

Carl Balsiger

Why named: On the 1951 list; his name was in her address book and his answers were evasive. Eli Frankel's 2025 book argues he did it and links him to a 1941 Kansas City murder.

Against: LAPD found no firm evidence; that murder file was never obtained.

No free photo found
Unverified

Dr. Patrick O'Reilly

Why named: A surgeon on the 1951 list, linked to two of Short's friends and the subject of allegations dating to 1939.

Against: 2023 research found he had lied about his name and birthplace, which undermines his credibility but does not clear him.

No free photo found
Discredited

George Knowlton (d. 1962)

Why named: His daughter Janice said in the early 1990s, from "recovered memories," that he killed Short.

Against: The lead detective said her claims did not fit the facts. A dig at the childhood home found nothing, and the autopsy found no pregnancy.

Latest suspect: Marvin Margolis

The same man is also named in our Zodiac file. This section covers what is specific to the Dahlia case.

Not an accusation. Margolis died in 1993 and was never charged. The newest claims come from an independent investigator and a 2026 book. No agency has confirmed them.

Why he is the current suspect

  • He fits more of the pattern than most. Mann says he is the only suspect who combines contact with Short, medical or anatomical experience, a history of disturbance, and access to a facility (a USC lab).
  • He was on the 1951 list, and LAPD treated him as a key suspect. He was one of her boyfriends.
  • A physical item is being checked. In May 2026 LAPD detective Marty Mojarro said a 1943 government fingerprint card, supplied by Baber and not previously held by police, "warrants further investigation."
  • The alibi is thin. It comes from his recent wife, and the 1947 report lacks details.

The case against the theory

  • LAPD reportedly interviewed and cleared him in 1947. Researcher Larry Harnish says the report lacks alibi details and that he stayed a suspect after leaving the state.
  • No published physical link. No fingerprint comparison result, DNA, or recovered item ties him to the body, the package, or a scene.
  • A 22-year gap and a different crime. Linking this case to the 1969 Zodiac killings is an extra, unproven step.
  • Family and expert disagreement. Some relatives reject the claim; some veteran detectives support it.

What would settle it

  1. Compare the 1943 card to every latent print the LAPD holds from 1947. The package, handbag, and shoe were wiped, so surviving prints may be few, and Mojarro said he could not comment on what exists.
  2. Test any retained biological evidence against a voluntary family sample.
  3. Release the unredacted autopsy and the 1949–50 DA files so independent examiners can check the claims.
  4. Verify his whereabouts for Jan 9–15, 1947 from employment, school, and military records.
Cold Case Checker's assessment

Plausible, untested

We put the chance Margolis killed Elizabeth Short at roughly 20%, the highest of any named suspect, because he matches the profile and a physical comparison is possible. That is far from a conclusion. The chance he was also the Zodiac is far lower, at roughly 3–5%, since nothing physical connects the two cases. A fingerprint comparison result would change both numbers.

Editorial judgment, not statistics. We will revise when evidence changes.

Our hypothesis

Editorial reading of public records. A working theory, not a finding, and it names no individual.

Working theory · confidence stated per point

An acquaintance with access to a private space and some anatomical skill, holding her for days

  1. She was held, not killed on the spot. She was last seen Jan 9 and found Jan 15. The body was washed and posed. That points to a place and several days.
  2. The killer knew how to handle a body. The cuts and the clean handling suggest medical, mortuary, or butchery experience, though that is an inference from descriptions and not a forensic finding.
  3. The killer wanted attention. The calls, the package, and the signed letters follow a pattern shared by later cases, including the Zodiac. That does not make them the same person.
  4. The best next step is records, not new theories. The unredacted autopsy, the DA files, and anything the LAPD still holds decide whether any claim can be tested.

Missing links

Questions raised by gaps in the public record. Some may already have been answered privately.

1. Where she was Jan 9–15

Nothing confirms where she spent six days. The Biltmore sighting is itself questioned. A careful reconstruction of every sighting, sourced to 1947 records, is not published.

2. The unredacted autopsy

The report has been withheld for nearly eight decades. A petition backed by a documentary team and reportedly by surviving family asks for its release.

3. What the LAPD still holds

A public inventory of 1947 evidence (the package, handbag, shoe, clothing) would show what could be tested. An LAPD detective declined to say what exists.

4. The 1949–50 DA and grand-jury files

The Hodel wiretap file surfaced in 2003. Other material may remain unreleased.

5. The 1951 list of 22

Re-examining each name against modern records, and releasing the list's basis, would clear or sharpen leads.

6. The motel claim

A documentary team says it found blood in a room at a motel that was not open in 1947. DNA results are pending and the claim is unverified. A public method and independent lab review would matter.

7. The phone call

A caller reached the Examiner. Notes of that call and any witness descriptions have not been analyzed publicly.

8. The Margolis fingerprint card

A comparison to every available 1947 latent print, with a published result, would settle a major claim.

How similar cases broke

Boston Strangler (Mary Sullivan)

In 2013, DNA from Sullivan's 1964 murder was matched through a relative to Albert DeSalvo. Lesson: 1960s evidence that was kept and stored properly can still produce answers.

Golden State Killer

Old crime-scene DNA matched relatives in a public database, leading to Joseph DeAngelo's arrest in 2018. Lesson: genealogy can bypass a dead-end suspect list.

What differs here

This evidence is from 1947 and was wiped, so little biological material may remain. Everything depends on what the LAPD kept.

Who holds the case & how to request records

Los Angeles Police Department

Open case; cold-case detectives, including Marty Mojarro (named in May 2026 reporting).

LA County Medical Examiner

Autopsy and death records. The full report has been withheld.

LA County District Attorney

1949–50 files, including the Hodel wiretap.

FBI

Fingerprint identification. Records via the FBI Vault or FOIA.

Sample public-records request

Under the California Public Records Act, I request copies of non-exempt records relating to the 1947 homicide of Elizabeth Short, including the autopsy report, evidence inventories, and any index of evidence. Please send electronic copies. If any part is withheld, please identify the exemption relied on and release all reasonably segregable material.

Agencies often withhold records of open investigations, so expect partial answers. For the FBI, use a FOIA request instead.

Remembering Elizabeth Short

She was a young woman far from home. Her family spent decades without answers, and her surviving sisters died without them.

If you know something, even something that seems small, contact the LAPD or tips.fbi.gov. We do not list memorial pages until we can confirm her family supports them.
