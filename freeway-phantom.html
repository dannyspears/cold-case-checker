Case file · The Freeway Phantom Murders
Updated November 16, 2026Status: unsolved
STATUSNo one has ever been charged. D.C. police and Prince George's County police still seek information; the MPD poster offers up to $150,000. Call 202-727-9099 or text 50411; tips can be anonymous.

The Freeway Phantom: six Black girls and young women were taken off the streets of Washington in 17 months and left beside the highways. Fifty-five years later, no one has been charged.

Between April 1971 and September 1972, six Black girls and young women, ages 10 to 18, were abducted from their neighborhoods in Washington, D.C. Most had been sent to a store, were walking home, or were taking a bus. Their bodies were found along highways and ramps in Southeast D.C. and in Prince George's County, Maryland, most of them strangled. A note found on the fifth victim was signed "Free-way Phantom," the name a newspaper had already given the killer. A task force of D.C., Maryland and federal investigators never charged anyone, and much of the file was later lost. The Metropolitan Police Department lists the case as open.

What happened

Details come from the Metropolitan Police Department's victim page and reward poster, Wikipedia's articles on the case and on Robert Askins (which summarize the Washington Post, the Washingtonian, Washington City Paper and 1970s newspapers), and All That's Interesting (Dec 2022). We could not open the Washington Post or Washingtonian ourselves.

Apr 25, 1971

Carol Spinks, 13

Sent to a 7-Eleven about half a mile away and taken on the way home. Found six days later on an embankment beside northbound I-295 behind St. Elizabeths Hospital; strangled (Wikipedia). The MPD poster gives Apr 21 as the abduction date; MPD's web page and Wikipedia say Apr 25.

Jul 8, 1971

Darlenia Johnson, 16

Taken on the way to a summer job at the Oxon Hill Recreation Center; a witness saw her in an old black car driven by a Black man. Found about 15 feet from where Carol was found; an anonymous caller had pointed police there (Wikipedia, citing the Washington Post, 1997).

Jul 27, 1971

Brenda Crockett, 10

Sent to a store. That night she called home crying, saying a white man had picked her up, then called again. Found the next morning beside U.S. 50 near the Baltimore-Washington Parkway in Prince George's County; strangled with a scarf (Wikipedia). Investigators believed the killer made her place the calls.

Oct 1, 1971

Nenomoshia Yates, 12

Walking home from a Safeway in Northeast about 7 p.m. Found within hours beside Pennsylvania Ave. in Prince George's County; strangled. A witness reported a blue Volkswagen (Wikipedia). The "Freeway Phantom" name first appeared in the press after her death.

Nov 15, 1971

Brenda Woodard, 18

Took a bus home about 11:30 p.m. Found hours later on a ramp to Route 202 near Prince George's County Hospital, stabbed and strangled, with a coat over her chest and a signed note in her pocket (Wikipedia, citing 1971 newspapers).

Sept 5, 1972

Diane Williams, 17

A Ballou High senior, last seen boarding a bus about 11:20 p.m. Found hours later beside I-295 just south of the District line; strangled (Wikipedia).

1970s

Task force

D.C. homicide and sex-squad detectives, Prince George's and Montgomery County police, Maryland State Police and the FBI worked the case (Wikipedia, citing an MPD poster and the Washington Post, 1980).

1974

Green Vega gang interviews

Detectives interviewed members of the "Green Vega" rape gang in prison. An informant named a man for one killing and gave a detail that was not public; he later stopped cooperating (Wikipedia, uncited passage).

1977-1978

Askins searched

Robert Askins is charged in an unrelated abduction and rape; his home is searched and his yard dug up for the Phantom case. Nothing links him; he is never charged with the killings (Wikipedia).

Apr 18, 2006

Reward poster

MPD issues a poster offering up to $150,000 (Commons copy of the MPD poster).

2009

Files lost

D.C. police acknowledge much of the case file and evidence, possibly including DNA, cannot be found (All That's Interesting, citing the Washingtonian, Jul 2009).

Apr 30, 2010

Askins dies

Robert Askins dies in federal prison at 91, still denying any role (Wikipedia, citing the Washington Post, 2018).

Jan 2021

Records withheld

MPD denies a records request because release could interfere with an enforcement proceeding (MPD FOIA decision 2018-054, search summary only; we could not open it).

Nov 2026

Still open

MPD's victim page still asks for information. We found no arrest or charge.

Suspects

No one has been charged. One man was publicly named as a suspect.

No one has been charged in any of the six deaths. Robert Askins is named because police searched his home for this case and the Washington Post reported it; that does not mean he was responsible. We do not name members of the Green Vega gang.

Robert Askins in a police lineup (date unknown). Image as used on Wikipedia (non-free; attributed there to the D.C. police through a records release, which we could not confirm). Never charged in these deaths. To request removal, use the contact form below.

Named suspect, died 2010

Robert Elwood Askins

Why named: A man with a 1938 poisoning conviction (later overturned) and a 1952 killing charge. In 1977-78, after an unrelated abduction and rape, police searched his home; a court paper there used "tantamount," the unusual word in the Phantom note, and colleagues said he used it often. His yard was dug up (Wikipedia, citing the Washington Post).

Against: Nothing physical was found; he was never charged with the killings and denied them until his death in 2010 (Wikipedia). No free photo of him exists.

The "Green Vega" gang

Why named: Men convicted of armed abductions and rapes near the Beltway in the early 1970s were treated as suspects; an informant gave a detail about one killing that was not public (Wikipedia).

Against: No evidence tied them to the six deaths (All That's Interesting). We do not name them.

Latest: Robert Askins (named suspect, never charged)

Askins is the most recent publicly named suspect who was never ruled out.

Never charged. He died in prison in 2010.

What is known

  • Police searched his rowhouse in 1978 and found a knife from another crime, photos of girls and young women, scarves, and a court paper using "tantamount" (Wikipedia: Robert Askins).
  • He was serving life for two 1970s abductions and rapes (Wikipedia).
  • Against: no physical evidence linked him; the witness descriptions do not clearly fit him; he denied it (Wikipedia; All That's Interesting).

What would settle it

  1. DNA. If any evidence survived (the 2009 Examiner report), a profile could be compared with Askins's and with national databases.
  2. The note. A fresh handwriting and language review.
  3. A witness. Anyone who knew who drove the girls away.
Cold Case Checker's assessment

One predator who knew Southeast D.C. and the highways

We put the chance that Askins was the killer at roughly 20%: the "tantamount" link is striking but thin, and nothing physical tied him. That is our judgment, not a finding. We put the chance of a public resolution within five years at roughly 5%: the files are incomplete and most witnesses are elderly or dead.

Our hypothesis

Editorial reading of public records. A working theory, not a finding, and it names no individual.

Working theory · confidence stated per point

A local predator with a car

  1. He knew the girls' streets. They were taken from Southeast, Northeast and Northwest neighborhoods while running errands or heading home.
  2. He had a car and knew the highways. Every body was left beside a major road on the D.C.-Maryland line.
  3. He controlled his victims. The calls home and the dictated note suggest a man who made girls do what he said.
  4. He may have known some of them. Handwriting analysis suggested Woodard wrote calmly; Darlenia's location was phoned in by someone with inside knowledge.
  5. Surviving evidence is the key. DNA, if any remains, is the only way to settle it now.

Solved cases

Local cases that moved after decades.

Carol Sue Klaber (Boone County, KY, 1976)

Investigators say a private genealogy investigation helped them identify Thomas Dunaway, who died in 1990, as the man who killed the 16-year-old. He cannot be indicted, so prosecutors sent a letter saying he would have been charged. The lesson: genealogy can name a killer after decades, even when no trial is possible.

Forensic genetic genealogy training

In 2024, local investigators were being trained in the technique, which has already helped solve Cincinnati-area cases, according to FOX19. The lesson: the tool is now local, so cases with usable DNA have a real path forward.

Golden State Killer

Old crime-scene DNA matched relatives in a public database and led to a 2018 arrest. The lesson: a usable sample can still work after decades.
