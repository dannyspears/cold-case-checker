The Cora Durham case: a young mother, a roadside, and a body moved from somewhere else.

On June 20, 1975, the body of Cora Durham, 27, a mother of a nine-year-old, was found nude in brush in what is now Deerfield Township, Warren County, Ohio. She had been strangled. Detectives believe she was killed elsewhere. No one has been charged.

Case file at a glance

What happened

Details come from a 2024 FOX19 report. Some points are one detective's account and are marked unverified.

About mid-June 1975

Last seen

Cora Durham, 27, is last seen alive at her house in the Norwood area. Her common-law husband says he had not seen her for about a week. No missing-person report is filed.

June 20, 1975

The discovery

Her body is found nude in brush near Irwin Simpson Road in what is now Deerfield Township, Warren County. Warren County deputies respond.

1975

The autopsy

Autopsy reports indicate death by asphyxiation from strangulation, and a struggle. There are no signs of sexual assault, per Detective Lt. Shaun Embleton. Very little physical evidence is recovered.

1975 and after

Investigators' belief

Investigators believe she was killed elsewhere and her body dumped in Warren County. Detective Embleton says she "took off after an argument," an unverified detail.

Feb 2024

A fresh look

Warren County detectives say they are reexamining the case, plan new interviews, and are considering links to other 1970s killings of women in the Tri-State area. One person they spoke with is in prison and did not confess to this case.

Victims

A mother whose life had taken a hard turn in her final years.

Died

Cora Durham, 27

A young mother of a nine-year-old son. She had eight siblings. Her sister says her life took a dark turn about four years before her death, involving domestic violence and drugs. Her mother died about 20 years before 2024 without answers.

Latest lead: a possible series of 1970s killings

Detectives are asking whether Durham's killing belongs to a wider pattern of 1970s killings of women across the Tri-State area.

Not an accusation. No suspect has been named. The series idea is a question detectives are exploring, not a finding.

What was said in 2024

  • Detectives are considering links to other 1970s killings of women in the Tri-State area, including Clermont County, Butler County, and Northern Kentucky.
  • They have spoken with suspects in those cases. One is still in prison and did not confess to this one.
  • They plan new interviews.

What would settle it

  1. Compare the cases. Place, method, victim type, and date for each 1970s killing.
  2. Retest any evidence across the cases for a shared donor.
  3. Re-interview people who knew Durham in the weeks before her death.
Cold Case Checker's assessment

A possible link, not yet a pattern

We put the chance that Durham's killing is linked to at least one other 1970s Tri-State killing at roughly 25%. The scene (a body dumped outdoors), the strangulation, and the era fit a pattern, but so do many other cases. A shared DNA profile, if any evidence survives, would settle it.

Working theory · confidence stated per point

Someone she knew or met, a killing elsewhere, and a body left far from home

  1. She was moved. The killing happened somewhere else, which means a vehicle and a place.
  2. A week passed. She was last seen about a week before she was found, which fits an unreported disappearance.
  3. Her life was difficult. Her sister describes domestic violence and drugs, which may point to people close to her.
  4. The series question is worth testing. Comparing the 1970s cases is the next step.

Solved cases

Local cases that moved after decades.

Carol Sue Klaber (Boone County, KY, 1976)

Investigators say a private genealogy investigation helped them identify Thomas Dunaway, who died in 1990, as the man who killed the 16-year-old. He cannot be indicted, so prosecutors sent a letter saying he would have been charged. The lesson: genealogy can name a killer after decades, even when no trial is possible.

Forensic genetic genealogy training

In 2024, local investigators were being trained in the technique, which has already helped solve Cincinnati-area cases, according to FOX19. The lesson: the tool is now local, so cases with usable DNA have a real path forward.

Golden State Killer

Old crime-scene DNA matched relatives in a public database and led to a 2018 arrest. The lesson: a usable sample can still work after decades.
