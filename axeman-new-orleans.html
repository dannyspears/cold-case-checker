The Axeman of New Orleans: a chiseled door panel, a famous letter, and a murky body count.

Between May 1918 and October 1919, someone attacked sleeping households in and around New Orleans with an axe or a razor, usually after chiseling out a back-door panel. Most targets were Italian immigrants. A letter promised to spare any house playing jazz. The attacker was never identified.

Cold Case Checker's conclusion · Oct 2026

Case file at a glance

The Axeman case is as much a history of a city's fear as a murder mystery. The documented attacks are few, the suspects were mostly arrested on weak grounds, and a famous letter may be a hoax. The honest answer is that nobody knows.

PeriodMay 1918 – Oct 1919
PlaceNew Orleans and Gretna, LA
Killed6–17 (sources differ)
Injured6–14 (sources differ)
WeaponAxe or straight razor
StatusUnsolved
  1. A signature entry method.The attacker usually chiseled a panel out of a back door and attacked sleeping victims with an axe or a razor. Robbery was not the motive.
  2. Most victims were Italian immigrants.The press suggested Mafia involvement with no evidence. Modern researchers doubt it.
  3. The "jazz letter" is famous and unproven.A letter published March 13, 1919 said the killer would spare homes where jazz was playing. Many homes hosted jazz that night and no murders were reported. Its authorship is unknown.
  4. Several people were wrongly accused.A father and son were convicted, then freed when their accuser admitted she lied. A grocer was tried and acquitted.

What happened

Dates come from period newspapers as summarized by later authors. The exact victim count is disputed.

May 23, 1918

Joseph and Catherine Maggio

Italian grocer Joseph Maggio and his wife are attacked in their home. Joseph dies. A bloody razor found at the scene belongs to his brother Andrew.

June 27, 1918

Louis Besumer and Harriet Lowe

The couple are attacked. Lowe is left partly paralyzed. Besumer is later charged with murder and acquitted on May 1, 1919.

Aug 5 and 10, 1918

Anna Schneider and Joseph Romano

Anna Schneider survives and recalls nothing. Joseph Romano dies two days after his attack.

March 10, 1919 · Gretna

The Cortimiglia family

Mary Cortimiglia is killed and her parents are injured. A father and son are later convicted on a false accusation.

March 13, 1919

The jazz letter

A letter published in the press, signed as from the killer, says he will spare any house where jazz is playing on a certain night. Many New Orleans homes host jazz that night, and no murders are reported.

Aug – Sept 1919

Steve Boca and Sarah Laumann

Both survive attacks with no memory of them.

Oct 27, 1919

The last attack

Mike Pepitone is killed in what is generally considered the last Axeman attack.

1919–1920s

Accusations and trials

Several men are arrested and released. The Jordanos, father and son, are convicted and later freed when Rosie Cortimiglia admits she falsely accused them.

Latest theory: Frank "Doc" Mumphrey

No suspect has been proven. The most developed modern theory comes from a scholar who studied the jazz letter and the city's music scene.

Not an accusation. The theory is an argument by one researcher. The man named died long ago and was never charged.

The theory

  • Richard Warner argues the chief suspect was Frank "Doc" Mumphrey, who also used the names Leon Joseph Monfre and Manfre.
  • His jazz business reportedly boomed after the city was pressured into hiring jazz bands, which is the pressure the letter created.
  • On this view, the "jazz letter" was not a joke but the point of the crimes.

What would settle it

  1. Prove the identity. Census, city directory, and court records for Mumphrey and Monfre.
  2. Place him at the scenes. Where he lived and worked on each attack date.
  3. Check the letter's source. Which paper received it, and in what handwriting.
Cold Case Checker's assessment

Clever, unproven

The jazz-promotion motive is the most interesting idea in the case, but it explains the letter, not the killings. We put the chance Mumphrey was the Axeman at roughly 8%. The chance that the jazz letter itself was a hoax or press invention is higher, roughly 50%.

Our hypothesis

Editorial reading of public records. A working theory, not a finding, and it names no individual.

Working theory · confidence stated per point

Fewer attacks than the legend says, a local offender, and a letter that is probably not his

  1. The core series is small. Only a handful of attacks share the chiseled-panel method. Expanding the count to 17 depends on loosely linked incidents.
  2. The offender knew the neighborhoods. The attacks clustered among small grocers and tradespeople, and the method needed time to work on a back door unobserved.
  3. The letter probably did not come from the killer. No pattern of boasting letters exists, and the "jazz" promise could not be enforced.
  4. The best test is a records audit. Match each documented attack against city arrest, parole, and release records for 1918–19.

Solved cases

The lesson from Villisca

In a 1912 case with no biological evidence, researchers turned to newspaper research and patterns. The same approach is the only realistic path here.

The lesson from the Jordanos

The only convictions in the case were later undone when an accuser recanted. Reliable records matter more than confessions.
