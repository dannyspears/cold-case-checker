The Amy Diesman case: eight minutes, three people in a store, and a killer who may have known her.

Before dawn on Sept 12, 1987, Amy Diesman, 19, a new mother working her first weeks as a third-shift clerk, was shot twice in the head at a Stop N Go on Ohio 125 in Pierce Township, east of Cincinnati. Police believe three people were in the store minutes earlier. No one has been convicted.

Case file at a glance

The Diesman case has an unusually tight timeline: at 5:27 a.m. three people were inside, and by 5:35 one was dead and one customer was gone. Police believe the killer knew her. Two grand jury indictments of a primary suspect were dismissed, and the case remains open.

What happened

Details come from a May 2023 FOX19 report and the Ohio Attorney General's cold-case listing. The police timeline is from the lead investigator.

Sept 1987

A new job

Amy Diesman, 19, a recent New Richmond High School graduate who planned to study nursing, has a four-month-old daughter. She has worked about a week as the third-shift clerk at a Stop N Go on Ohio 125 in Pierce Township.

Sept 12, 1987 · 5:27 a.m.

Three people in the store

Police believe three people were inside: Diesman, a female customer, and a male customer. The woman leaves; the man remains.

5:35 a.m.

The discovery

The male customer is gone and Diesman is dead. A customer entering the store finds her. She was shot twice in the head, once behind the ear and once behind the head, with a .25 caliber weapon. There is no sign of sexual assault and no indication of robbery.

1987

The evidence

Investigators photograph the scene and recover a .25 caliber bullet lodged in a package of donuts and a WEBN sweatshirt. A suspect sketch is made; police now say it is no longer useful. Gun sale records are checked and items are sent to a ballistics lab with no reported results.

After 1987

Indictments

A primary suspect, unnamed, is indicted twice by a grand jury. Both sets of charges are dismissed.

2007

A new lead investigator

Lt. Bryan Burke begins working the case. The Ohio Bureau of Criminal Investigation later assists through a cold-case grant.

May 2023

Evidence shared

Pierce Township police share evidence publicly, hoping to generate tips. No DNA is found on the sweatshirt.

Victims

A young mother in her first weeks at a new job.

Latest lead: why the indictments were dismissed

The most important open question is not who, but why a twice-indicted primary suspect was never tried.

Not an accusation. The primary suspect is not named here and has not been convicted. Charges were dismissed twice.

What is known

  • Police say the killer likely knew Diesman, which is also the conclusion of the Stop N Go internal review.
  • A grand jury indicted the primary suspect twice and the cases were dismissed. The report calls the reasons unclear.
  • In 2023 police released evidence to generate tips and asked for information at the Pierce Township police tip line.

What would settle it

  1. Review the dismissed cases. Court records in Clermont County should show why each was dismissed.
  2. Identify the male customer and the female customer through records and memories.
  3. Test the bullet and any retained items with current ballistic and DNA methods.
Cold Case Checker's assessment

A killing by someone she knew, with the evidence problem unsolved

We put the chance that the killer knew Diesman at roughly 70%, in line with police and the company's own review. We put the chance that the unnamed primary suspect committed the crime at roughly 35%, because two grand juries indicted, but charges were dismissed and no trial tested the evidence.

A personal killing at work, done quickly, by someone she knew

  1. It was fast. The window between the last customers and her death was minutes.
  2. Robbery was not the point. Police saw no indication of robbery.
  3. The killer knew her schedule. She had worked there only a week, which suggests someone who knew she was on that shift.

Solved cases

Local cases that moved after decades.

Carol Sue Klaber (Boone County, KY, 1976)

Investigators say a private genealogy investigation helped them identify Thomas Dunaway, who died in 1990, as the man who killed the 16-year-old. He cannot be indicted, so prosecutors sent a letter saying he would have been charged. The lesson: genealogy can name a killer after decades, even when no trial is possible.

Forensic genetic genealogy training

In 2024, local investigators were being trained in the technique, which has already helped solve Cincinnati-area cases, according to FOX19. The lesson: the tool is now local, so cases with usable DNA have a real path forward.

Golden State Killer

Old crime-scene DNA matched relatives in a public database and led to a 2018 arrest. The lesson: a usable sample can still work after decades.
