James Heath case: a 56-year-old man beaten to death on Delta Avenue, in a case almost no one has written about.

On June 22, 2010, James Heath, 56, was beaten to death at 298 Delta Avenue in Cincinnati, according to the Ohio Attorney General. The address sits near Eastern Avenue in Columbia Tusculum, on the city's east side. No one has been charged.

What would settle it

  1. A witness. Anyone near the low end of Delta Avenue or Eastern Avenue that day who saw an argument or someone leaving.
  2. Forensic evidence. DNA under fingernails or on clothing, retested with methods that did not exist in 2010.
  3. The people around him. Who he lived with, worked with or spent time with in June 2010.
Cold Case Checker's assessment

A personal confrontation with someone he knew or met that day

We put the chance that he knew his attacker at roughly 60%: a fatal beating is close and personal, and most such killings involve an argument between people who know each other. That is our judgment, not a finding. We put the chance of a charge within five years at roughly 8%: there is no public lead, but a beating often leaves DNA, which the city's 2024 cold case task force could retest.

Working theory · confidence stated per point

A close-range confrontation, probably with someone he knew

  1. A beating is personal. The attacker was within arm's reach. That usually means an argument, not a stranger passing by.
  2. It left a trace. Fists or a blunt object leave blood and skin. If evidence was kept, modern DNA testing could identify someone now.
  3. No robbery reported. Nothing in the state listing says anything was taken; it does not say either way.
  4. Nobody told the story. With no news coverage and no family appeal we could find, the people who knew him in 2010 may never have been asked publicly. They are the missing link.
  5. A fresh look is possible. Cincinnati's 2024 cold case task force could review the file and resubmit any physical evidence.

Solved cases

Local cases that moved after decades.

Carol Sue Klaber (Boone County, KY, 1976)

Investigators say a private genealogy investigation helped them identify Thomas Dunaway, who died in 1990, as the man who killed the 16-year-old. He cannot be indicted, so prosecutors sent a letter saying he would have been charged. The lesson: genealogy can name a killer after decades, even when no trial is possible.

Forensic genetic genealogy training

In 2024, local investigators were being trained in the technique, which has already helped solve Cincinnati-area cases, according to FOX19. The lesson: the tool is now local, so cases with usable DNA have a real path forward.

Golden State Killer

Old crime-scene DNA matched relatives in a public database and led to a 2018 arrest. The lesson: a usable sample can still work after decades.
