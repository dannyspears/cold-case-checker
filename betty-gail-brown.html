The Betty Gail Brown case: a student strangled in her car on campus, and a confession no one could prove.

On Oct 26 or 27, 1961, Betty Gail Brown, 19, a Transylvania University sophomore, was found strangled with her own bra in her parked car in front of Old Morrison on the Lexington campus. Years later a drifter confessed. His trial ended with a hung jury, and he was never retried.

Case file at a glance

Police printed and questioned hundreds of people and never found a motive. The confession matched details printed in newspapers years earlier, which undercut it. The man who confessed had an unreliable memory, died in 1980, and was never convicted. The case is still open.

What happened

Details come from a University of Kentucky article and a Transylvania student newspaper feature. They disagree on some dates.

Oct 26 to 27, 1961 · about midnight

Last seen

After a biology study session at Forrer Hall, Brown gets into her car around midnight. Sources give the date of her death as Oct 26 or 27.

Oct 27, 1961 · about 3 a.m.

Found

Police find her dead in the front seat of her parked car in front of Old Morrison, strangled with her own bra. The autopsy finds no sexual assault.

1961 to 1965

The investigation

Police take testimony, polygraphs and fingerprints from hundreds of people and follow out-of-state leads. A waitress says she saw Brown with a woman at a diner. Several students say Brown was not there, and the lead ends.

About 1965 to 1966

The confession

Alex Arnold Jr., a drifter, confesses while in jail on other charges. He says he attacked Brown and a woman in her car and strangled Brown. His account closely matches details printed in newspapers earlier. Her parents deny she was involved with a woman.

Trial (date not stated)

No verdict

Arnold pleads not guilty. His testimony is inconsistent. The trial ends in a hung jury and he is never retried.

1980

Arnold dies

Arnold dies at 49.

2010 and 2011

Reopened

The student newspaper reports police reopening the case in 2010. In 2011 police ask the defense lawyer whether a convicted serial strangler in Ohio, Kentucky and Michigan could be responsible. He says he does not believe so. Nothing links that man to the case.

Nov 2017

A book

A former defense lawyer, later a University of Kentucky law professor, publishes a book on the case. He remains unsure whether Arnold was the killer.

Latest lead: whether the confession can be tested

The central question is whether the 1960s confession was real knowledge or recycled newspaper detail.

Not an accusation. The man who confessed is dead and was never convicted.

What is known

  • The University of Kentucky says the case is open and that new evidence has surfaced periodically, most recently in 2010, but none has panned out.
  • The confession matched details printed in newspapers earlier.
  • The man who confessed died in 1980.

What would settle it

  1. Re-test any retained evidence such as the bra and clothing for DNA and genetic genealogy.
  2. Compare the confession with unpublished facts in the police file.
  3. Find the 1961 diner witness's account and check whether a woman was ever identified.
Cold Case Checker's assessment

A killing by someone who knew where she parked, with the confession a false trail

We put the chance that Arnold was the killer at roughly 25%, because his account matched newspaper details and he was inconsistent. We put the chance of a solution within five years at roughly 3%, because it depends on surviving evidence from 1961.

Solved cases

Local cases that moved after decades.

Carol Sue Klaber (Boone County, KY, 1976)

Investigators say a private genealogy investigation helped them identify Thomas Dunaway, who died in 1990, as the man who killed the 16-year-old. He cannot be indicted, so prosecutors sent a letter saying he would have been charged. The lesson: genealogy can name a killer after decades, even when no trial is possible.

Forensic genetic genealogy training

In 2024, local investigators were being trained in the technique, which has already helped solve Cincinnati-area cases, according to FOX19. The lesson: the tool is now local, so cases with usable DNA have a real path forward.

Golden State Killer

Old crime-scene DNA matched relatives in a public database and led to a 2018 arrest. The lesson: a usable sample can still work after decades.
