Updated November 8, 2026Status: open, unsolved

Desiree Cherry case: "I'll be back in a minute," she told her 14-year-old son one night in May 2006. Four days later she was found stabbed and burned in a Brooklyn marsh.

Late on May 23, 2006, Desiree Cherry, 36, stepped out of her apartment on Schenectady Avenue in Crown Heights, Brooklyn, after telling her teenage son she would be right back. She never came home. On Saturday, May 27, two bicyclists found a woman's body a few feet from a footpath in the Fresh Creek Preserve, a salt marsh near Starrett City and Canarsie. She had been stabbed in the neck and chest, and her body had been burned. On May 31 dental records showed it was Desiree.

Case file at a glance

Desiree Cherry, a mother from St. Lucia who danced at New Jersey clubs, left home at night in May 2006 saying she would be right back, and was found four days later stabbed and burned in a marsh miles away. Her family said the 71st Precinct was slow to act on their missing-person report. Her friends spoke of stalkers, and police sources said detectives were looking at men she had dated and a patron. No one was ever named or charged, and we found no coverage after June 2006. Our view: she went out to meet someone she knew, who killed her elsewhere and drove her body to the marsh.

Desiree Cherry, 36

Forum theory, unverified

A serial killer

Why named: Some Websleuths members think her murder may be connected to other killings of women in the New York area: Tanya Rush (East New York, found dismembered in a suitcase off the Southern State Parkway, 2008, unsolved), "Cherries" (an unidentified woman whose torso was found in a suitcase in Mamaroneck, 2007, still unidentified in 2024), Erica McDaniel (a dancer found in a burned suitcase in Brooklyn in 2013, Newsday) and Thalia Stathis (found off the Southern State Parkway, 2008). The common threads they point to: dancers or sex work, burning, stabbing, suitcases, East New York and the Belt and Southern State parkways (Websleuths, 2017).

Against: This is a theory from an online forum, not from police. No police or press report we found links her case to any other. Thalia Stathis's case was solved: her live-in boyfriend was charged in 2008 and sentenced to 40 years (NJ.com, 2010; NJ appeals court, 2018). Rex Heuermann, the Gilgo Beach killer, pleaded guilty in 2026 to seven murders and admitted an eighth; she is not among them. The other cases differ in method (dismemberment, suitcases) from hers.

She went out to meet someone she knew, who killed her elsewhere and drove her body to the marsh

We put the chance that she went out to meet someone she knew, rather than a stranger, at roughly 70%: she expected to be back in a minute, which fits a short, planned meeting. We put the chance that she was killed elsewhere and driven to the marsh at roughly 70%: the preserve is miles from her home, and burning the body suggests time and planning. We put the chance of a charge within five years at roughly 10%: there has been no public word on the case since 2006. These are our judgments, not findings.

Our hypothesis

Editorial reading of public records. A working theory, not a finding, and it names no individual.

Working theory · confidence stated per point

A short trip out, and someone she knew

  1. She expected to be gone a minute. "I'll be back in a minute" is what you say when you are stepping out to meet someone nearby: a car outside, a quick errand, a short talk. It suggests she knew who she was meeting. This is our reading.
  2. She did not go to the marsh on her own. The Fresh Creek Preserve is miles from Crown Heights, on the edge of the city by the Belt Parkway. Whoever left her there almost certainly drove her, alive or dead.
  3. The fire was meant to hide something. Burning a body takes time and fuel. It destroyed evidence and delayed her identification by days. That points to someone who feared being linked to her, more than to a random attacker.
  4. The stabbing was close and personal. Wounds to the neck and chest are face to face. Together with the burning, the killing looks like anger, not robbery.
  5. Detectives looked where we would. In 2006 they looked at men she dated and at a patron, and her friends spoke of stalkers. We name no one; none of them has been publicly identified or charged.

Solved cases

Local cases that moved after decades.

Carol Sue Klaber (Boone County, KY, 1976)

Investigators say a private genealogy investigation helped them identify Thomas Dunaway, who died in 1990, as the man who killed the 16-year-old. He cannot be indicted, so prosecutors sent a letter saying he would have been charged. The lesson: genealogy can name a killer after decades, even when no trial is possible.

Forensic genetic genealogy training

In 2024, local investigators were being trained in the technique, which has already helped solve Cincinnati-area cases, according to FOX19. The lesson: the tool is now local, so cases with usable DNA have a real path forward.

Golden State Killer

Old crime-scene DNA matched relatives in a public database and led to a 2018 arrest. The lesson: a usable sample can still work after decades.
