The Cleveland Torso Murders: twelve official victims, three names, no killer.

Between 1934 and 1938, the remains of at least a dozen people turned up in and around Cleveland's Kingsbury Run. Only three victims were ever identified. Public Safety Director Eliot Ness took on the case, and the killer was never caught.

Case file at a glance

This is the oldest case in our set that science is about to revisit. The suspect list is short and weak, and the real prize is identifying the nine victims who never got names, which could finally show who they were and where they came from.

StatusUnsolved
  1. Most victims were poor and unnamed.Many were drifters living in the Flats shantytowns. Only Edward Andrassy, Florence Polillo, and, tentatively, Rose Wallace were identified.
  2. Eliot Ness could not stop it.Ness oversaw the hunt, ordered the shantytowns burned in 1938, and was taunted by the killer, who left remains in view of his office. The killings stopped without an arrest.
  3. The suspect list is thin.Frank Dolezal confessed, recanted, and was found hanged in custody. Dr. Francis Sweeney, who failed two polygraphs, was never charged.
  4. Science has returned.In 2024, medical examiners and the DNA Doe Project began exhuming unidentified victims to identify them through genetic genealogy.

What happened

Dates come from the Wikipedia summary of period records. Counts differ between sources.

Sept 1934

The "Lady of the Lake"

A woman's remains are found on the Lake Erie shore. Some researchers count her as the first victim; the official count begins later.

Sept 1935

Edward Andrassy

The remains of Edward Andrassy are identified by fingerprints. He had a 1931 Cleveland Police record. Another man, never identified, is found nearby.

Jan 1936

Florence Polillo

Florence Polillo, victim No. 3, is identified by fingerprints.

1936 – 1938

More victims

Further remains are found in and around Kingsbury Run and the Flats. The killer usually decapitated victims and often dismembered them; most male victims were castrated. The press calls him the "Mad Butcher of Kingsbury Run."

Summer 1938

Ness and the shantytowns

Ness orders the Flats shantytowns searched and burned. The raid finds nothing incriminating. The killer leaves remains in view of Ness's City Hall office and later sends him postcards.

1939

Frank Dolezal

Frank Dolezal is arrested, confesses, and retracts. He is found hanged in his cell. He is later exonerated posthumously.

1944

Willie Johnson executed

Willie Johnson, who had been considered a suspect, is executed for a different murder.

1950

A possible later victim

Robert Robertson's remains are found; some researchers count him as a later victim.

1964

Sweeney dies

Dr. Francis Sweeney, a World War I veteran and alcoholic whom Ness had interviewed, dies.

2024

Exhumations begin

The Cuyahoga County Medical Examiner's Office and the DNA Doe Project begin exhuming unidentified victims to identify them using genetic genealogy.

What is happening

  • In 2024, the Cuyahoga County Medical Examiner's Office and the DNA Doe Project began exhuming unidentified victims.
  • The goal is to use genetic genealogy to give names to victims who have been unnamed for about 90 years.
  • We found no published identifications from this project as of Oct 2026.

Why it matters

  • Knowing who the victims were and where they lived could show how the killer found them.
  • A pattern of origins, such as one neighborhood, could narrow the suspect pool.
  • Descendants may be able to give family names back to the dead.

What would settle it

  1. Name the victims through genealogy and records.
  2. Compare their last known whereabouts and associates.
  3. Test any surviving evidence against known suspects' descendants where possible.
Cold Case Checker's assessment

Names first, suspects later

We put the chance that the exhumations identify at least one more victim in the next five years at roughly 55%. We put the chance that the killer is ever named at roughly 5%. The most likely suspect named in public, Dr. Francis Sweeney, has a roughly 10% chance in our view, because the evidence is a polygraph and circumstance.

Our hypothesis

Editorial reading of public records. A working theory, not a finding, and it names no individual.

Working theory · confidence stated per point

A killer who preyed on people no one would report missing, and a gap that identification could close

  1. He chose people who would not be missed. Most victims were drifters or poor, living in the Flats. That is why so few were identified.
  2. He had the means and a private place. Dismemberment and disposal took time, tools, and somewhere to work unobserved.
  3. He stopped, or was stopped, around 1938. The killings ended soon after the Flats shantytowns were cleared, possibly because his hunting ground disappeared.
  4. The next break is a name, not a clue. Identifying even a few victims may reveal where he found them.
Single offender for the core cases Medical or butchery skill Victims identified in 5 years Sweeney responsible Killer named

Solved cases

DNA Doe Project successes

The organization has used genealogy to name long-unidentified remains across the country. The lesson: names can come back after decades if remains were kept.

Golden State Killer

Genealogy led to a 2018 arrest of a killer from the 1970s–80s. The lesson: it needs killer DNA, which may not exist here.

What differs here

Most were poor and unnamed. Efforts to give them names, and descendants the chance to claim them, are the most meaningful work still being done.

If you think a relative may have disappeared in Cleveland in the 1930s, contact the Cuyahoga County Medical Examiner's Office or the DNA Doe Project. We do not list memorial pages until we can confirm families support them.
