The Linda Pierson case: an 18-year-old, a riverbank, and children playing hide-and-seek.

In the evening of Nov 17, 1966, Linda Pierson, 18, left her home in Dayton, Kentucky, a small town on the Ohio River across from Cincinnati. Hours later children playing a game found her body near the river. She had been killed somewhere else. No one has been charged.

Case file at a glance

Pierson's case is a 60-year-old Northern Kentucky homicide with a small set of facts: a teenager who left home at about 7 p.m., a discovery by children, and a body moved to the riverbank. The state police still say someone knows more than has been told.

What investigators found

Evidence reportedly indicated she had been raped and strangled elsewhere, then dumped nearly naked by the river. A Kentucky State Police trooper later said she had been killed by "some type of string instrument," possibly rubber bands. That is a description, not an official finding.

Cold Case Checker's assessment

A local crime with a local answer

We put the chance that the killer lived in or near Dayton, Kentucky at roughly 70%, because she was killed elsewhere and moved a short distance to a place near her home. The chance of resolution in the next five years is, in our view, low, roughly 5%.

Working theory · confidence stated per point

Someone local took her, killed her elsewhere, and left her where children would find her

  1. The killer had a vehicle and a place. She was killed elsewhere and moved to the riverbank.
  2. The dump site was local. It is near her home, which suggests familiarity with Dayton.
  3. Time is the problem. Most people who knew the case in 1966 are gone, so documents matter more than memories.
  4. The best chance is retained evidence. If anything survives, genealogy could help.

Solved cases

Northern Kentucky has solved cases like this.

Carol Sue Klaber (Boone County, KY, 1976)

Investigators say a private genealogy investigation helped them identify Thomas Dunaway, who died in 1990, as the man who killed the 16-year-old. He cannot be indicted, so prosecutors sent a letter saying he would have been charged. The lesson: genealogy can name a killer after decades, even when no trial is possible.

Forensic genetic genealogy training

In 2024, local investigators were being trained in the technique, which has already helped solve Cincinnati-area cases, according to FOX19. The lesson: the tool is now local, so cases with usable DNA have a real path forward.

Golden State Killer

Old crime-scene DNA matched relatives in a public database and led to a 2018 arrest. The lesson: a usable sample can still work after decades.
