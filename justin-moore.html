The Justin Moore case: a 19-year-old waiting for a ride on California Avenue, shot twice from behind, and a mother who says, "We know who they are."

On September 18, 2007, Justin Brandon Moore, 19, known as "Biggie," was sitting on the steps outside an address on California Avenue in Cincinnati's Bond Hill neighborhood, near a corner store, waiting to be picked up. He was shot twice in the back of the head and died. The Attorney General gives the address as 1330 California Avenue. No one has been charged.

Case file at a glance

His mother, Robin Moore, says the shots came from across the street and from the top of a building, and she has asked publicly "who paid who to do it." In 2020 she told the killers, "We know who they are." She holds a candlelight vigil at the spot every year and counsels young children affected by murder. In 2019, Cincinnati police detectives John Horn and Kelly Best were working the case.

What is known

  • He was shot twice in the back of the head while sitting and waiting for a ride.
  • His mother says the shots came from across the street and a rooftop, asks who paid for it, and says the family knows who did it.
  • Detectives John Horn and Kelly Best were on the case in 2019; Cincinnati formed a cold case task force in 2024.

What would settle it

  1. Confirm the firing position. Rooftop or across the street, from the original scene work.
  2. Identify who he was waiting for and who knew he would be on those steps.
  3. Turn what the family knows into evidence: a witness willing to testify, a phone record, or a weapon.
Cold Case Checker's assessment

A planned ambush by people who knew where he would be, possibly arranged by someone else

We put the chance that the killing was planned and targeted at roughly 75%: he was shot from behind while sitting still, and his mother describes a shooter across the street and on a rooftop. We put the chance that someone else arranged or paid for it at roughly 35%; that rests on his mother's words, not police findings. We put the chance of a charge within five years at roughly 10%, higher than most cases here because the family says the killers are known.

Our hypothesis

Editorial reading of public records. A working theory, not a finding, and it names no individual.

Working theory · confidence stated per point

An ambush set up around a young man waiting for a ride

  1. He was a stationary target. Sitting on steps, waiting to be picked up: someone could know where he would be and when.
  2. The shots came from behind, and maybe from above. Two to the back of the head; his mother says from across the street and a rooftop. That points to a planned ambush, not a passing argument.
  3. His mother believes it was paid for. "Who paid who to do it." If true, at least two people share the secret: the shooter and whoever arranged it.
  4. The family says it knows who did it. The missing piece is evidence a prosecutor can use, which usually means a witness who will testify.
  5. Two detectives and, since 2024, a task force. The case has had attention; what it needs is someone to speak.

Solved cases

Local cases that moved after decades.

Carol Sue Klaber (Boone County, KY, 1976)

Investigators say a private genealogy investigation helped them identify Thomas Dunaway, who died in 1990, as the man who killed the 16-year-old. He cannot be indicted, so prosecutors sent a letter saying he would have been charged. The lesson: genealogy can name a killer after decades, even when no trial is possible.

Forensic genetic genealogy training

In 2024, local investigators were being trained in the technique, which has already helped solve Cincinnati-area cases, according to FOX19. The lesson: the tool is now local, so cases with usable DNA have a real path forward.

Golden State Killer

Old crime-scene DNA matched relatives in a public database and led to a 2018 arrest. The lesson: a usable sample can still work after decades.
