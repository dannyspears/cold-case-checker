Shirley Vaughn case: a single mother left home for the bus to work one January morning in 1997. She never arrived. Ten weeks later she was found strangled in the Mill Creek.

On a cold January morning in 1997, Shirley Vaughn, 36, a divorced mother, left her home on Glenmore Avenue on Cincinnati's west side to catch the bus to work. Detectives do not think she ever got on it: a passenger remembered hearing a man offer her a ride. When she did not show up at work, her boss reported her missing. On March 25, 1997, a maintenance worker found her body washed up along the Mill Creek near the CSX Queensgate rail yard. She had been strangled.

Case file at a glance

Her daughter, Christy, was 11. That morning they argued, and for the only time she did not watch her mother get on the bus. In 2014 she gave cold case detectives a letter her mother had written to a friend, which mentions a man she went out with. A man in the case file had argued with Shirley about their relationship and failed a polygraph test; police said they wanted to find him and question him again. He has never been named or charged. Nearly three decades on, no one has been.

What would settle it

  1. The ride. Anyone who was at her bus stop on Glenmore Avenue that January morning, or saw a car pick a woman up there.
  2. The man in the letter. Anyone who knew who Shirley was seeing in late 1996 and early 1997.
  3. The creek. Anyone who saw a car stop along the Mill Creek or near the Queensgate yard that winter.
  4. Evidence kept from 1997. Her clothing or the ligature, if kept, could be tested with methods that did not exist then.
Cold Case Checker's assessment

She accepted a ride from someone she knew, before the bus came

We put the chance that she left the bus stop in someone's car, rather than boarding the bus, at roughly 70%: detectives believe she never boarded, and a passenger heard a man offer a ride. We put the chance that the killer was someone she knew at roughly 65%: she was a self-described loner with a fixed routine, a stranger's offer is easier to refuse, and police have focused on a man she dated. We put the chance of a charge within five years at roughly 10%, because the water destroyed most forensic evidence. These are our judgments, not findings.

Our hypothesis

Editorial reading of public records. A working theory, not a finding, and it names no individual.

Working theory · confidence stated per point

She got into a car she trusted

  1. She never boarded the bus. That is what detectives believe, and a passenger heard a man offer her a ride (WKRC, 2014). The likeliest way she left the stop is in someone's car.
  2. She probably knew the driver. This is our reading. She called herself "pretty much a loner" and kept a strict routine of work and home. On a January morning, a familiar face offering a warm car is far easier to accept than a stranger.
  3. Strangulation is close and personal. A ligature killing takes time and physical closeness. It fits anger between people who know each other more than a random robbery, and nothing suggests she was robbed.
  4. The creek was chosen. The Mill Creek runs through industrial land and rail yards, out of sight. Whoever left her there had a vehicle and likely knew the area. The water then washed away most forensic evidence.
  5. The man in the file fits, but is not proven. A man who argued with her about their relationship failed a polygraph, and her own letter mentions a man she was seeing. Neither fact is evidence of murder, and police have named no one. We name no one.

Solved cases

Local cases that moved after decades.

Carol Sue Klaber (Boone County, KY, 1976)

Investigators say a private genealogy investigation helped them identify Thomas Dunaway, who died in 1990, as the man who killed the 16-year-old. He cannot be indicted, so prosecutors sent a letter saying he would have been charged. The lesson: genealogy can name a killer after decades, even when no trial is possible.

Forensic genetic genealogy training

In 2024, local investigators were being trained in the technique, which has already helped solve Cincinnati-area cases, according to FOX19. The lesson: the tool is now local, so cases with usable DNA have a real path forward.

Golden State Killer

Old crime-scene DNA matched relatives in a public database and led to a 2018 arrest. The lesson: a usable sample can still work after decades.
