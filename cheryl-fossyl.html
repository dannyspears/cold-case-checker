The Cheryl Fossyl case: a 1977 murder, a grand jury with no indictment, and a civil jury that named two men.

Cheryl Fossyl, 16, disappeared from Georgetown, Ohio on June 4, 1977. Her remains were found in two places in the following weeks. Brown County reopened the case in 2001 and a grand jury heard 40-plus witnesses without indicting anyone. In 2007 her siblings won a civil verdict against two men. No one has been criminally charged.

Case file at a glance

The Fossyl case shows the gap between civil and criminal proof. A jury decided in 2007 that two men were responsible for her death, but prosecutors said there was no forensic evidence linking anyone, no living eyewitnesses, and no confession. Murder has no statute of limitations, so the criminal case could be reopened.

What happened

Details come from a Maysville Ledger Independent report on the 2002 grand jury and a FOX19 report on the 2007 civil verdict. Some dates are inferred, as marked.

She disappears

Cheryl Fossyl, 16, disappears from Georgetown, Ohio.

First discovery

Four boys fishing near a bridge over Straight Creek on US 68 find part of her remains. Sources differ on the exact date.

More than two weeks later

Second discovery

Her nude body is found in a creek bed in Adams County.

Jan 2001

The case is reopened

Brown County Sheriff Dwayne Wenninger reopens the case shortly after taking office. One secondary account says a deathbed statement from a woman prompted the reopening; we could not confirm that and treat it as unverified.

Dec 17, 2001 (year inferred)

Exhumation

Her body is exhumed from Tate Township Cemetery in Bethel and taken to Dayton for DNA testing and a full autopsy, which was not done in 1977.

Early 2002 (year inferred)

Special prosecutor

County commissioners ask for a special prosecutor. The county prosecutor steps down from the case after the family's lawyer alleges conflicts. A Hamilton County assistant prosecutor is appointed.

2002 (year inferred)

Grand jury

A special grand jury meets six times starting May 8 and hears from more than 40 witnesses. It returns no indictments, finding insufficient evidence for a murder trial.

Nov 15, 2007

Civil verdict

A jury rules that two men sued by her brothers and sister are responsible for her murder. The report says there was never enough evidence for a criminal case.

Latest lead: why a civil jury and a grand jury disagreed

The most important open question is what the civil jury heard that the grand jury did not, or weighed differently.

Not an accusation. The two men from the civil case are not named here, and no criminal charge has been filed. We found no reporting after Nov 2007.

What is known

  • A grand jury heard more than 40 witnesses and returned no indictments.
  • Prosecutors said there was no forensic evidence linking anyone, no living eyewitnesses, and no confession.
  • A civil jury found two men responsible in Nov 2007. No criminal charge followed, according to the FOX19 report.
  • Murder has no statute of limitations, and prosecutors said in 2002 a significant credible development could reopen the case.

What would settle it

  1. Obtain the civil trial record from the Brown County court, which is public.
  2. Re-test the exhumation samples with current DNA methods and genetic genealogy.
  3. Ask the prosecutor to review the civil evidence against the criminal standard.
Cold Case Checker's assessment

A killing by people local to Georgetown that the evidence could not support in a criminal court

We put the chance that the 2007 civil verdict identified at least one person involved at roughly 60%, because a jury heard evidence we cannot see. We hold it below the criminal standard because a grand jury that heard 40 witnesses declined to indict and no forensic link was found.

A group killing by locals, never proven to a criminal standard

Solved cases

Local cases that moved after decades.

Carol Sue Klaber (Boone County, KY, 1976)

Investigators say a private genealogy investigation helped them identify Thomas Dunaway, who died in 1990, as the man who killed the 16-year-old. He cannot be indicted, so prosecutors sent a letter saying he would have been charged. The lesson: genealogy can name a killer after decades, even when no trial is possible.

Forensic genetic genealogy training

In 2024, local investigators were being trained in the technique, which has already helped solve Cincinnati-area cases, according to FOX19. The lesson: the tool is now local, so cases with usable DNA have a real path forward.

Golden State Killer

Old crime-scene DNA matched relatives in a public database and led to a 2018 arrest. The lesson: a usable sample can still work after decades.
