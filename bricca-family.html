The Bricca family murders: a missing knife, an unlocked door, and sixty years of silence.

In late September 1966, neighbors found a young couple and their four-year-old daughter stabbed to death in their home in Green Township, outside Cincinnati. There was no sign of forced entry. The knife was never found. No one has ever been charged.

Case file at a glance

The Bricca case is a quiet-street mystery with a short list of physical items and a long list of rumors. The most useful fact is that biological evidence was kept, and it has been retested at least once. Whether it can still name anyone depends on how well it survived.

What happened

Dates and times come from the Ohio Attorney General, local news, and a 2008 Cincinnati Magazine feature. Where sources differ, we say so.

Sunday, Sept 25, 1966

The last day anyone saw him

Jerry Bricca attends 10 a.m. Mass, works at the Monsanto plant, and stops at a store around 8 p.m. He puts the trash cans out at the curb and is seen by a neighbor walking her dog. Linda and four-year-old Debbie are in the family's recreation room.

Monday, Sept 26

Signs that something is wrong

The trash cans stay at the curb. Newspapers pile up and lights stay on. Neighbors later recall the family's dogs were silent that day.

Tuesday, Sept 27 · about 10 p.m.

The discovery

Jerry has not reported to work for two days. Two neighbors find the front door unlocked and notice a strong odor. They call police. All three family members are found dead in their bedrooms. There is no sign of forced entry.

Sept 1966

The scene

Jerry and Linda had been bound, likely with tape, and the material was removed and never found. Jerry had a sock in his mouth held by a small piece of medical tape. Debbie, 4, was found in her bedroom in pajamas. Some drawers were open and Jerry's wallet was missing, but police did not believe robbery was the motive. A carving knife from the dining set matching the wounds was missing.

1966 – 1967

The first investigation

Hamilton County Sheriff's detectives conduct hundreds of interviews. Lt. Herbert Vogel leads. He travels to Chicago to examine a possible link to the murder of Valerie Percy that September; no connection is found. In 1967 a prosecutor says police have narrowed the field to one suspect who hired a lawyer; current officials contradict that claim.

1980s – 2008

Reviews

The case is reviewed in the 1980s and 1990s. In 1999 Capt. Lloyd Zoellner reviews it; in 2008 he says it is "still an active investigation" and that no forensic evidence ties anyone to the crime.

March 2013

State attention

The Ohio Attorney General's Unsolved Homicides Initiative publishes the case and asks the public for information. It notes the killer may be deceased.

2016 – 2022

Fresh eyes

On the 50th anniversary, detectives Brian Williams and Douglas Todd review the file. In 2020 evidence is retested, and the Hamilton County prosecutor says an ancestry group found no match. In 2022 Williams says detectives have no sole suspect and no specific motive.

Sept 2026

60 years

Local news marks the anniversary. The sheriff's office still treats the case as open and unsolved.

Latest theory: Fred Leininger

Authorities have named no suspect. The most developed public theory comes from a 2010s book by a local historian who had access to the case file.

Not an accusation. Fred Leininger died in 2004 and was never charged. The idea that he was involved is one author's theory. The sheriff's office does not comment on uncharged suspects.

The theory

  • J.T. Townsend, author of "Summer's Almost Gone," suggests veterinarian Fred Leininger could have been involved, possibly with others who knew the family.
  • Linda had worked for Leininger for three days in the week before the killings.
  • Townsend notes that tape on Jerry's face was identified as medical or veterinary tape, and that Leininger's hair samples are in an evidence bag.

The claims and how strong they are

The case against the theory

  • No forensic link. No published comparison ties Leininger to the scene, the hair, or the cigarette butts.
  • Common materials. Medical tape is not unique to veterinarians.
  • Three days. A brief job does not show a motive or access.
  • He cannot respond. He died in 2004 and was never interviewed under oath, as far as we found.

What would settle it

  1. Compare the retained hair and the 1966 biological samples to a voluntary relative's DNA.
  2. Test the cigarette butts and the hair in Linda's hand with current methods.
  3. Check the tape. Compare it with medical and veterinary tape sold in Ohio in 1966.
  4. Interview Linda's former co-workers from the clinic, if any are alive.
Cold Case Checker's assessment

A lead worth testing, not believing

We put the chance that Leininger was involved at roughly 10%. The link is real but thin, and rests on one author's reading of the file. We put the chance that more than one person was involved at roughly 40%, because binding two adults and sedating dogs is hard for one offender. A DNA comparison would change both numbers.

Our hypothesis

Editorial reading of public records. A working theory, not a finding, and it names no individual.

Working theory · confidence stated per point

Someone the family knew, or someone who was let in, who took time and planned the restraints

  1. There was no forced entry. That points to someone the family knew or let in, or someone who walked in through an unlocked door.
  2. The killer or killers were prepared. Tape and restraints were brought, and a knife from the home was used. The binding material was taken away.
  3. Robbery was not the point. A wallet was taken, but drawers were disturbed and the family was left; police did not think it was robbery.
  4. The best hope is retained evidence. The 2020 retest and ancestry search found no match. A fuller search, or a different database, may do better.

Solved cases

Boston Strangler (Mary Sullivan)

In 2013, DNA from a 1964 victim was matched through a relative to Albert DeSalvo. The lesson: properly kept 1960s evidence can still give answers.

Golden State Killer

Old crime-scene DNA matched relatives in a public database and led to a 2018 arrest. The lesson: database choice and sample quality matter.

What differs here
