The Chicago Tylenol murders: seven deaths, one extortion letter, no killer.

In late September 1982, seven people in the Chicago area died after taking Extra-Strength Tylenol capsules laced with potassium cyanide. The poisoning changed how every product is packaged. The person who did it has never been charged.

Cold Case Checker's conclusion · Oct 2026

Case file at a glance

This is a case where the evidence points at one man and the proof stops short. James Lewis sent the extortion letter and was convicted of that, but never of the poisonings. He died in 2023, and the question now is whether anything left can tie him, or anyone else, to the bottles.

What happened

Dates come from police, court, and news records. Disputed items are marked.

Sept 28–29, 1982

The first deaths

Mary Kellerman, 12, takes a capsule and dies the next day. Three members of the Janus family, Adam, Stanley, and Theresa, take pills from the same bottle on Sept 29 and die. Investigators link both bottles to lot MC2880.

Sept 29–30, 1982

Three more victims

Mary McFarland, 31, Paula Prince, 35, and Mary Reiner, 27, also die after taking tainted capsules.

Early Oct 1982

The recall

The recall widens to all Extra-Strength Tylenol capsules sold in the Chicago area, regardless of lot. Johnson & Johnson issues a nationwide recall on Oct 5, covering about 31 million bottles. Tainted bottles are found at several Chicago-area stores, including Jewel, Osco, Walgreens, Dominick's, and Frank's Finer Foods.

Fall 1982

The extortion letter

A letter to Johnson & Johnson demands $1 million to stop the killings. It is traced to James W. Lewis.

1983

Lewis convicted of extortion

Lewis is convicted of extortion and sentenced to 10 years. He is not charged with the poisonings. A second man, Roger Arnold, who told police he had potassium cyanide, is held several times but never charged with them; he is later convicted of an unrelated killing.

1983 and after

Law and packaging change

Tamper-evident seals and tamper-resistant packaging become standard, a federal crime for product tampering is created, and many companies drop powder-filled capsules for caplets.

2009 – 2011

Renewed federal effort

In 2009 federal agents search Lewis's Massachusetts home. Court documents say investigators believed he was responsible but lacked enough evidence to charge him. In 2010, DNA from Lewis and from Arnold's exhumed body does not match DNA on the bottles. In 2011 the FBI seeks Ted Kaczynski's DNA; he denies ever possessing potassium cyanide.

July 9, 2023

Lewis dies

James Lewis, 76, is found dead at home in Cambridge, Massachusetts. Police say the death is not suspicious; the medical examiner cites a pulmonary embolism.

2025

New coverage

A Chicago Tribune investigation and a Netflix docuseries revisit the case. The Tribune reports investigators had come across findings described as "chargeable" and circumstantial.

Sept 2026

The Idaho link

The Ada County Sheriff's Office in Idaho identifies a man known as the "Unknown Wanderer," found dead under a pew at Sacred Heart Catholic Church in Boise on Dec 4, 1982, of cyanide poisoning. Arlington Heights police say they are reviewing it. Illinois State Police say the task force vetted similar information years ago and found no evidentiary link.

Cold Case Checker's assessment

Interesting coincidence, not yet a lead

Cyanide deaths are rare, and a Dec 1982 church death fits the timeframe, so we do not dismiss it. But no agency has found a physical or travel link. We put the chance it is connected at roughly 5%. We put the chance that James Lewis was the poisoner at roughly 40%, because of the extortion letter and the federal belief, but with no forensic proof.

Our hypothesis

Editorial reading of public records. A working theory, not a finding, and it names no individual.

Working theory · confidence stated per point

A deliberate act by someone who could handle cyanide and knew the Chicago stores, then used the crisis to extort

  1. The poisoner worked in Chicago stores, not at the plant. Poison reached bottles in different stores and different lots, which points to someone moving between stores in the days before Sept 28.
  2. The extortion may or may not be the same person. Someone could have written the letter after the news broke, which is the central uncertainty. We treat the letter and the poisoning as separate questions.
  3. Cyanide access narrows the field. The poison is common in industrial and lab settings. A list of people with legitimate access in 1982 has not been published.
  4. The bottles are the best untried evidence. Modern genealogy-grade DNA work on any preserved bottle, label, or capsule could produce a profile that the 2010 tests could not.
One person did the poisonings Lewis was the poisoner Idaho death is connected Preserved evidence can still be tested Solved within five years

Missing links

Questions raised by gaps in the public record. Some may already have been answered privately.

1. Store-by-store timeline

Who restocked, cleaned, or worked in each tainted store in the days before Sept 28? A published reconstruction is missing.

2. Surveillance and receipts

Some stores had cameras or receipts. Whether any were reviewed against employee lists is not public.

3. The cyanide

No public source says where the poison came from. Industrial purchasing and lab records from 1982 are rarely searched.

4. DNA on the bottles

A re-test with genealogy-grade methods on any preserved bottle, if possible. The 2010 tests used older methods.

5. The letter

Modern stylometric and handwriting work on the extortion letter has not been published.

6. Lewis's whereabouts

A complete timeline of Lewis in the weeks around Sept 28, 1982 has not been made public.

7. The Boise death

Release of the vetting file Illinois says it completed, and a chemical comparison if samples exist.

8. The 2025 "chargeable" findings

The Tribune reported circumstantial findings described as chargeable. Details are not public.

Solved cases

Excedrin poisonings (Washington, 1986)

Stella Nickell was convicted of murdering Susan Snow and Bruce Nickell by spiking capsules. The lesson: some poisoners are caught through the poison and the purchase trail.

Sudafed poisonings (Washington, 1991)

Joseph Meling was sentenced to life. The lesson: later cases with clearer product trails ended in convictions.

What differs here

The poisoner appears to have acted in stores, not at a plant, which leaves a wider and older trail.
